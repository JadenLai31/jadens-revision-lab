## 1 Energy and its units

Energy is the capacity to do work or cause change. In the introductory mechanical sense, work involves a force causing displacement. Energy is not a force, a substance that disappears, or simply how awake someone feels.

The SI unit of energy is the **joule, J**. A kilojoule is 1000 joules:

- kJ → J: multiply by 1000.
- J → kJ: divide by 1000.

**Worked example:** A process requires 2.75 kJ. In joules this is 2.75 × 1000 = **2750 J**. If two requirements are 2.75 kJ and 1.2 kJ, their difference is 1.55 kJ = **1550 J**.

Read a graph in this order: the requested category or time → the correct axis value → its unit → the required operation → any conversion. A correct conversion cannot rescue a value read from the wrong age or trial.

**Source:** S, opening notes; E, Q6–9.

## 2 Forms of energy

| Form | What it describes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Kinetic | Energy of motion | A rolling trolley |
| Gravitational potential | Energy due to position in a gravitational field | A book above the floor |
| Elastic potential | Energy stored by deformation | A compressed spring |
| Chemical | Energy associated with chemical substances | Food or a charged battery |
| Nuclear | Energy associated with atomic nuclei | A nuclear energy change |
| Thermal | Energy associated with particles’ motion and interactions | A warmed brake pad |
| Electrical | Energy transferred electrically in a circuit | A source supplying a motor |
| Sound | Energy transferred by vibrations through a medium | A vibrating speaker producing sound |
| Light | Energy transferred by electromagnetic waves | Sunlight reaching a panel |

An object can have more than one form at the same time. A falling ball has kinetic energy and, while still above the chosen zero level, gravitational potential energy. A stationary battery can have chemical energy despite having no kinetic energy relative to the room.

The sheet’s categories are useful at this level. Its elaboration correctly cautions that light, sound and thermal energy should not all be described as the kinetic energy of an entire moving object.

**Source:** S, Types of energy and glossary; E, Q1–5. Nuclear energy is in S but is not tested in the two supplied examples.

## 3 Kinetic energy

**Eₖ = ½mv²**, where m is in kilograms and v in metres per second. The answer is in joules.

At fixed speed, doubling mass doubles kinetic energy. At fixed mass, doubling speed makes kinetic energy four times greater, because speed is squared. When two factors change, multiply their effects.

**Worked example:** A 600 g toy travels at 5 m/s.

1. Convert mass: 600 g = 0.6 kg.
2. Substitute: Eₖ = ½ × 0.6 × 5².
3. Square first: 5² = 25.
4. Eₖ = **7.5 J**.

**Reasoning example:** If mass triples and speed halves, the energy multiplier is 3 × (½)² = ¾. The new kinetic energy is 75% of the original.

Do not decide which of two objects has more kinetic energy from speed alone if their masses differ. Compare the complete expression.

**Source:** S, Formulas; E, Q10–14. The formula calculation is supported by S; E mainly assesses the relationships qualitatively.

## 4 Gravitational and elastic potential energy

Near Earth, **Eᵍ = mgh**, using m in kg, g ≈ 9.8 N/kg, and vertical height h in metres above a stated reference level. Use the value of g supplied in an assessment; this pack uses 9.8 N/kg unless stated otherwise.

**Worked example:** A 3 kg object is raised 80 cm.

80 cm = 0.8 m. The gain in GPE is 3 × 9.8 × 0.8 = **23.52 J**.

At fixed g, GPE is proportional to mass and height. Doubling either alone doubles GPE; doubling both quadruples it. Height needs a reference: use the same zero level for comparisons. A horizontal move at unchanged height does not change GPE in this simple model.

Elastic potential energy is stored when an object is stretched or compressed. For the same spring or band within its elastic range, greater deformation stores more elastic energy. Releasing it can transfer energy to a moving object. The sources do not require a spring-energy formula, so none is added to the core formula list.

**Source correction:** E Q16’s diagram gives unstretched length 20 cm, A = 25 cm and B = 30 cm. The later “50 cm” in its model answer conflicts with the diagram and earlier answer. Use 30 cm. Length and extension are different: A extends by 5 cm and B by 10 cm from the 20 cm original length.

**Source:** S, Formulas/Types of energy; E, Q15–20.

## 5 Transfers transformations and conservation

An **energy transfer** follows energy from one object or place to another. An **energy transformation** follows a change in energy form. A single event can involve both.

Use these sentence patterns:

- Transfer: “___ energy is transferred from **object A** to **object B**.”
- Transformation: “___ energy is transformed into ___ energy as ___ happens.”

**Example:** A moving foot kicks a stationary ball. Energy is transferred from the foot to the ball. As the ball rises, some of its kinetic energy becomes gravitational potential energy. Simply writing “kinetic energy” does not identify the transfer.

**Solar-storage chain:** light → electrical → chemical potential in battery → electrical → useful light from lamp. A real device also transfers energy into other forms, often thermal energy.

The **law of conservation of energy** says energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can change form and be transferred. Define the system you are discussing. A ball may lose mechanical energy, while the ball, floor and surrounding air together conserve total energy.

**Worked energy budget:** Initially a spring stores 90 J. After release, 65 J is kinetic energy of the car and 25 J is thermal energy gained within the system. The final total is 65 + 25 = **90 J**. A bar chart must include both final forms, with consistent labels and scales.

**Source:** S, Conservation; E, Q21–30.

## 6 Useful wasted and efficient energy transfer

“Useful” depends on the intended purpose. Heat is useful in a heater but is often unwanted in a device intended to produce motion. “Wasted” energy is still conserved; it has been transferred into forms less useful for that purpose.

**Efficiency (%) = useful output energy ÷ total input energy × 100.**

If there is no remaining stored energy, input = useful output + other outputs.

**Worked example:** A motor receives 400 J and gives 280 J of useful motion.

- Other outputs: 400 − 280 = **120 J**.
- Efficiency: 280/400 × 100 = **70%**.

Check that the chosen numerator is useful output. Reversing the fraction can produce an impossible efficiency above 100% for the stated complete energy accounting.

### Rebound investigations

During a bounce: GPE decreases as kinetic energy increases during falling; some kinetic energy becomes elastic energy while the ball and surface deform; some returns to motion and then GPE during rebound. Thermal energy, sound and other motions account for reduced recovered mechanical energy.

The sheet uses **rebound efficiency ≈ rebound height ÷ drop height × 100**.

**Worked example:** Release height 140 cm; rebound height 91 cm. Rebound percentage = 91/140 × 100 = **65%**.

**Added explanation of assumptions:** To interpret this as a recovered GPE fraction, use the same unchanged ball, approximately constant g, and consistent centre-of-mass heights measured above the relevant contact reference. Then m and g cancel in mghᵣ/mghᵈ. Release without an extra push. This measures recovered GPE over the whole fall–bounce–rise event, not automatically the isolated efficiency of contact with the floor. Air resistance and other processes can also contribute.

Two ball types can be compared by their percentages without claiming that the higher-percentage ball has more total energy: their masses or initial inputs may differ. Throwing a ball down supplies extra initial kinetic energy and changes the energy accounting.

**Source:** S, Energy Efficiency and rebound elaboration. Detailed experimental methods in the practice bank are added practice.

## 7 Electricity and circuit essentials

A simple working circuit needs an energy source and a complete conducting path. A **load** transfers electrical energy into other forms. Metal wires contain mobile electrons; a battery supplies energy rather than supplying all the circuit’s electrons from scratch.

- **Closed circuit:** a complete path supports sustained current under the stated source conditions.
- **Open circuit:** a break interrupts sustained current in that path.
- **Short circuit:** an unintended low-resistance bypass can carry excessive current.
- **Conductor:** allows charge to move easily, such as copper.
- **Insulator:** strongly resists charge movement, such as the plastic covering of wires.

In external metal wires of a DC battery circuit, electron drift is from negative towards positive. Conventional current is defined from positive towards negative. Label arrows explicitly.

Core symbols to recognise include a cell/battery, connecting wire, lamp, switch, resistor, ammeter and voltmeter. A cell is represented by one long and one short parallel line; a battery commonly has repeated pairs. The STILE examples label a single-pair source “battery”; this pack accepts “single-cell battery” where appropriate. The supplied sheet also shows LEDs, diodes, variable resistors, fuses, thermistors and LDRs; these are supplementary symbol recognition, with depth not confirmed by the tests.

**Source:** S, Electricity/diagrams; C, Q1–10 and Q18.

## 8 Current voltage resistance and meters

| Quantity | Meaning | Unit | Measurement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current I | Rate of charge flow | ampere, A | Ammeter in series |
| Voltage V | Energy transferred per unit charge between two points | volt, V | Voltmeter in parallel across a component |
| Resistance R | Opposition to current | ohm, Ω | Related to V and I through the stated model |

Voltage as a “push” is an analogy, not a force definition. One volt means one joule per coulomb. One ampere means one coulomb passing per second. The core tasks use these meanings without introducing extra charge-calculation requirements.

An ammeter measures current passing through it, so insert it into the relevant path. A voltmeter compares two points, so attach it across the component. Do not connect an ammeter directly across a source: its low resistance can create a bypass.

Electrical heating involves transfer of energy to a material through interactions involving charge carriers and its atomic structure. Charge is not consumed. Do not infer that increasing resistance always increases heating: the result depends on what else is held constant.

**Source:** S, Voltage/Current/Resistance; C, Q3–7 and Q17. The precision about the analogy follows S’s elaboration.

## 9 Ohm’s law

**V = IR; I = V/R; R = V/I.** For an ohmic conductor under constant conditions, voltage and current are proportional. State relevant assumptions, particularly temperature.

| Find | Example | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Current | V = 8 V; R = 40 Ω | I = 8/40 = 0.2 A |
| Voltage | I = 0.3 A; R = 20 Ω | V = 0.3 × 20 = 6 V |
| Resistance | V = 12 V; I = 0.04 A | R = 12/0.04 = 300 Ω |

At constant resistance, doubling voltage doubles current. At constant voltage, doubling resistance halves current. Avoid describing these relationships without their fixed-variable conditions.

For an **I-versus-V graph**, read a non-zero point and calculate R = V/I. Its slope is I/V, so the slope is the reciprocal of resistance. For a **V-versus-I graph**, the slope is V/I, which is resistance. Check axes before using a slope. A straight line through the origin supports a constant-resistance model over the measured range; it does not prove that the model applies at every temperature or voltage.

**Source:** S, Ohm’s law; C, Q11–16 and Q19. The graph-axis distinction is an added explanation supporting C Q16.

## 10 Series parallel AC and DC

| Feature | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Paths | One loop | Several branch paths |
| Current | Same through each series component | Divides among branches; supply current is their sum |
| Voltage | Component drops add to supply voltage | Each branch across the supply has its voltage |
| One branch/component fails open | Only loop breaks | Other branches can still work |
| Practical trade-off | Simple wiring; no independent path | Independent control; more wiring and potentially greater total current |

Equal series resistors share voltage equally; unequal ones need not. Parallel branches have equal voltage but need not have equal currents. A break in a **shared supply wire** stops all branches. Predictions of unchanged branch voltage use an ideal or adequately regulated source; real sources can have limitations.

**Worked examples:** Two series components across 12 V have drops of 5 V and **7 V**. Two parallel branches carrying 0.15 A and 0.25 A give total current **0.40 A**.

**DC** flows in one direction; **AC** periodically reverses. The sheet’s Australian mains example is 50 Hz: 50 complete cycles per second. For sinusoidal AC there are two direction reversals per cycle, so 100 reversals per second. This does not mean every AC source operates at 50 Hz. Practical tasks in this pack are models or low-voltage classroom scenarios, not mains experiments.

**Source:** S, Current and Types of circuits. These topics are not directly tested in the supplied STILE examples.

## 11 Investigations and design

The STILE energy assessment includes a robot design process and a spring/car investigation. The detailed fair-test terminology and rebound methods below are **added practice explanations** rather than confirmed assessment requirements.

- **Independent variable:** deliberately changed factor.
- **Dependent variable:** measured outcome.
- **Controlled variable:** factor kept consistent to support a fair comparison.
- **Repeatability:** consistency of results from repeated measurements under the same method and conditions.
- **Validity:** whether the design and measurements support the question and conclusion.
- **Accuracy:** closeness to the correct/reference value; precision concerns measurement resolution or consistency, depending on context.

For a release-height experiment, change release height and measure first rebound height. Keep the ball, surface and release method consistent. Use a scale and a consistent reference; video can help locate the maximum rebound. Repeat trials, calculate a mean, and describe spread. Do not discard an inconvenient result without an evidence-based reason.

**Worked summary:** Rebounds of 61, 65 and 63 cm give mean (61 + 65 + 63)/3 = **63 cm** and range 65 − 61 = **4 cm**. Repeats help show random variation. They do not remove a ruler’s systematic zero error.

Engineering design is iterative: establish performance criteria → develop prototypes → test under comparable conditions → evaluate evidence → improve → retest. A robot that is fast but unstable may fail its design criteria.

For a spring launching the same car, measuring initial speed gives a direct comparison of kinetic energy at fixed mass. Stopping distance is a proxy that also depends on friction, slope and wheel behaviour. E Q31’s wording about “kinetic energy of springs” should be interpreted carefully: a compressed stationary spring initially stores **elastic potential energy**.
